## Justification of own product business
Startup Business ADRs — Scientific Data Infrastructure for Life Sciences
Before the ADRs: one important framing difference from the consulting ADRs.
A startup is not trying to land one engagement. It is trying to own a market category — to become the default answer when a specific buyer has a specific problem. Veeva owns commercial CRM in pharma. Medidata owns clinical EDC. Flatiron owns oncology real-world data. The strategic question is: what is the unsolved infrastructure problem in life sciences that your startup can own, with sufficient market size to build a venture-scale business?
The DMD project answers a narrower question than that. It demonstrates the approach. The ADRs must answer the startup question.

ADR-S1: What market to enter
Context
Life sciences data infrastructure has produced multiple category-defining companies. When large pharmas replatform — as they have in commercial activities and clinical trials — it has yielded category kings such as Veeva and Medidata, with a combined value of over $35 billion. Scribd Each of these companies entered a specific, previously underserved segment of the pharma data stack and became the default platform for it. The question for a new entrant is: which layer of the stack remains unowned?
The three replatformed layers are:

Commercial CRM → Veeva (owns it)
Clinical trial EDC and operations → Medidata / Veeva Vault (contested but mature)
R&D lab data → TetraScience (building the category, Series C stage)

The layer that is conspicuously absent: the scientific data layer that sits between raw trial/genomics data and the analytical and regulatory outputs — the layer where biological meaning is encoded into data models. This is not an EDC problem. It is not a LIMS problem. It is a schema problem: the data exists, but it is structured to reflect the source system, not the science.
Decision
Enter the scientific data modelling and infrastructure market for precision medicine and rare disease gene therapy programmes, specifically the problem of encoding biological meaning into clinical and genomic data architectures.
Not genomics sequencing platforms (DNAnexus, Seven Bridges own that). Not clinical EDC (Medidata/Veeva). Not commercial CRM (Veeva). Not lab R&D data (TetraScience). The specific gap is the translational data layer — the join between molecular biology and clinical data that turns raw information into scientifically valid, queryable, regulatory-grade datasets.
Rationale
The market size is real. Global R&D labs spend more than $300 billion annually on research and development, with $177 billion in life sciences alone. TetraScience estimates $15 billion of that life sciences spend is focused on contending with data challenges via manifestly suboptimal approaches. Tetrascience Your startup is not competing for the whole $15 billion — but even a fraction of the segment that specifically concerns clinical-genomic data integration is a large enough market to build a venture-scale business.
The precedent proves the pattern. Veeva was not trying to compete with Salesforce broadly — it was solving the specific problem that Salesforce's generic CRM didn't encode pharmaceutical industry logic (regulatory submissions, sample management, validated systems). The result was a $30+ billion company. TetraScience is to Snowflake what Veeva was to Salesforce — the highly focused vertical domain expert, against the backdrop of a horizontal and distracted gorilla. Scribd Your startup's thesis is the same pattern applied to the translational data layer: general-purpose data platforms (Databricks, Snowflake) don't encode biological meaning; a domain-specific platform can own that layer.
The rare disease and gene therapy segment is the beachhead. The global gene therapy rare disease market was valued at $12 billion in 2025, growing at 18% CAGR, with over 1,200 active gene therapy clinical trials worldwide. Market Intelo These programmes are data-intensive, mutation-specific, and scientifically demanding in a way that generic data platforms cannot address. They are also pre-competitive: companies in this space have shared infrastructure needs (mutation classification, eligibility mapping, endpoint harmonisation) that no single company will build for itself. That is the classic market structure for a platform company.
Recent precedent confirms investor appetite. Delineate (YC-backed) processes existing clinical trial research into specialised datasets, working with two of the largest pharmaceutical companies to help them get to the next phase of trials faster by creating the largest dataset on a drug class ever constructed. Y Combinator That is a direct comp — a startup whose product is scientifically structured clinical data. The market exists; the category is forming.
Competitive positioning
You are not competing with Veeva, Medidata, or TetraScience — you occupy a different layer. You are competing with internal data engineering teams, with generic consultancies, and with the absence of any product at all. The moat is domain specificity: a data model that correctly encodes reading frame rules, exon-skipping eligibility, mutation nomenclature normalisation, and biomarker definitions cannot be built by a general-purpose platform. It requires someone who understands the biology.

ADR-S2: Why now
Context
Market timing for a startup is different from market timing for a consultant. A consultant needs a client today. A startup needs a market that is large enough to build into over 3–7 years. The question is whether the structural forces that will make this market large are already in motion — not whether a single client is ready to pay today.
Decision
The window is open because the replatforming wave in life sciences has reached the translational data layer, but no category-defining company has yet emerged to own it.
Rationale
Force 1: Replatforming is a demonstrated pattern with proven outcomes. As of early 2026, Veeva holds approximately 80% global market share in life sciences CRM and has grown to serve more than 1,500 customers with annual revenues approaching $3.2 billion. IntuitionLabs The industry has shown it will coalesce around a single platform for each layer of the stack, and it will pay premium prices for domain specificity. The translational layer has not yet had its Veeva moment.
Force 2: The gene therapy pipeline is creating a new class of buyer. Investment in cell and gene therapies reached $15.2 billion in 2025 — 30% growth compared to 2023. IntuitionLabs These companies face data infrastructure requirements that did not exist five years ago: mutation-to-therapy linkage, long-term follow-up data architecture, endpoint consistency across trials. They cannot use the same data stack as an oncology company — the biology is different in kind, not just degree. The buyer is new; the incumbent solutions don't fit.
Force 3: Regulatory pressure is creating an urgency signal. Regulators require structured long-term follow-up extending up to 15 years for many gene therapies, creating an extended regulatory and operational commitment. Avalerehealth Translarna's withdrawal and the Elevidys controversy have made regulatory data quality an existential concern for clinical-stage gene therapy companies. A platform that solves this problem is not a nice-to-have; it is infrastructure that protects the programme.
Force 4: AI adoption requires better underlying data, not just better models. The current AI hype cycle is causing some companies to jump straight into experimentation without ensuring they have the foundations to support trustworthy output — which will lead to wrong results and undermine trust in AI. A. K. Madan Every pharma company is investing in AI. None of them can realise AI's value on data that doesn't encode scientific meaning. The translational data layer becomes the foundation on which AI actually works — which makes it a prerequisite, not a product feature.

ADR-S3: Competitive landscape
Context
A startup needs to understand not just who exists today, but how the category will evolve and where defensibility comes from over time.
Decision
The competitive risk is not from existing players but from well-funded horizontal platforms (Databricks, Snowflake, Palantir) deciding to build domain-specific vertical layers. The moat must be built from scientific depth, not from platform capability alone.
The landscape in detail
Horizontal data platforms (Databricks, Snowflake): These companies have the engineering scale but not the domain knowledge. Databricks has worked with leading pharmaceutical and healthcare companies such as Regeneron Genetics Center, which paired de-identified genetic data with electronic health records to uncover drug discovery insights. Databricks But Databricks is a general-purpose platform — it provides the infrastructure; it cannot encode reading frame rules or mutation classification logic. That is the gap. The risk is that Databricks decides to build a life sciences vertical on top of its platform. The mitigation is that domain specificity at the level of molecular biology is not something a horizontal platform builds — it requires scientific expertise that is not in their talent base.
Clinical data management incumbents (Medidata, Veeva Vault): These platforms own clinical operations data — EDC, CTMS, safety. They do not own the scientific schema layer. Medidata's AI strategy focuses on trial feasibility, site selection, and synthetic control arms Life Sciences Review — operational problems, not scientific data modelling problems. The opportunity is in the space these platforms explicitly do not occupy: the layer between raw trial data and the biological interpretation of that data.
Specialist genomics platforms (DNAnexus, Seven Bridges): These platforms handle sequencing data pipelines and variant calling. They are strong at the genomics engineering layer but weak at the clinical integration layer — they don't natively join genomic variant data to trial eligibility criteria, biomarker definitions, or regulatory endpoints. Your startup sits at exactly that join.
Emerging direct comps:

Delineate (YC) processes clinical trial research into specialised datasets. Y Combinator Closest direct competitor. Small, early, YC-backed. Their existence validates the market; their narrowness (clinical trial data only, not genomic integration) defines the differentiation opportunity.
Owkin: Integrates biomedical images, genomics, and clinical data using ML. Bigger company, broader scope, not focused on data infrastructure but on model outputs. Different buyer and different problem.

The defensibility logic: Domain expertise compounds. A data model that correctly encodes DMD mutation-to-therapy eligibility, once built and validated by the patient community, becomes increasingly difficult to replicate. The scientific schema is not just code — it is encoded biological knowledge that required scientific judgement to produce. That is not something a horizontal platform or a generic data engineering team can copy without rebuilding the domain expertise from scratch.

ADR-S4: Key assumptions the startup bet rests on
A startup's assumptions are more consequential than a consultant's because the cost of being wrong is not a lost engagement — it is months or years of misdirected effort and capital.
The five bets, with honest confidence levels
Bet 1: Clinical-stage gene therapy biotechs will pay recurring subscription fees for a scientific data platform, not just one-time consulting services.
Confidence: Medium. This is the hardest assumption to validate without customers. The consulting model proves willingness to pay for the capability. The platform model requires proving that the need is recurring, not one-time. The evidence in favour: data infrastructure is ongoing — mutation registries update, trial data accumulates, endpoints evolve. The risk: early-stage biotechs are capital-constrained and may prefer to pay a consultant once rather than subscribe to a platform indefinitely. As an emerging biotech, your RWE needs are likely sporadic and don't require a full-time resource Peritia — which cuts both ways. Validation path: start with consulting engagements, identify which outputs clients want to access repeatedly, and build the subscription product around those recurring access patterns.
Bet 2: The scientific schema for monogenic rare diseases is sufficiently generalisable to support a multi-disease platform, not just a DMD-specific product.
Confidence: High. The underlying data model — mutation classification by exon, reading frame, and therapy class; trial eligibility encoded as computable rules; biomarker definitions aligned to endpoints — is the same pattern across SMA, Friedreich's ataxia, Wilson's disease, and all other monogenic diseases. The disease content changes; the architecture does not. DMD is the first instantiation, not the whole product.
Bet 3: The market will not be won by a large platform player acquiring or copying the capability before your startup reaches defensible scale.
Confidence: Medium. The acquisition risk is real — Roche acquired Flatiron Health for oncology data; Siemens acquired Dotmatics for $5.1 billion in July 2025 IntuitionLabs for R&D lab software. The mitigation is that the scientific depth required to build the translational data layer is not something acquirable in a single bolt-on. It requires years of domain knowledge and community trust — particularly in rare disease, where patient registries and advocacy groups are central to the data ecosystem. Build community trust before you are acquirable.
Bet 4: The regulatory environment continues to demand data quality and long-term follow-up from gene therapy programmes.
Confidence: High. This is structural, not cyclical. The shift from scientific experimentation to operational orchestration — coordinating shared infrastructure, aligning follow-up expectations, and sequencing launches to avoid system overload — is now central to gene therapy developers' role. Avalerehealth Regulatory requirements for 15-year follow-up data are not going away. If anything they are tightening, as post-market evidence gaps for early approvals become visible.
Bet 5: Starting as a consulting business and transitioning to a product is a viable path, not a trap.
Confidence: High, with a specific risk to manage. The consulting-to-product transition is a well-documented startup path — it provides customer discovery, domain credibility, and early revenue. The risk is that consulting cash flow creates organisational inertia that delays the product transition. The mitigation is to be explicit about the transition trigger from day one: consulting engagements generate the schema; the schema becomes the product; the product replaces the per-engagement model. Set a clear milestone — "after three consulting engagements in the same disease category, we ship a product" — and hold to it.
